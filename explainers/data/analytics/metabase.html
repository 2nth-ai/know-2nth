The BI tool people
actually open.

Metabase is the open-source BI tool that optimises for the non-analyst. Point-and-click questions, dashboards a finance manager can build unsupervised, and a SQL editor underneath for when that runs out. The thing to understand before you commit is the version number: a 0.x build is AGPL, a 1.x build is the commercial one, and they are the same release.

01 · What it is

Self-service BI that assumes you cannot write SQL.

Most BI tools are built for analysts and then marketed as self-service. Metabase genuinely starts from the other end: the primary interface is a question builder that walks someone through picking a table, filtering it, grouping it and seeing a chart, without typing anything. The SQL editor exists, is good, and is not where a new user lands.

That design choice determines where Metabase wins and loses. It wins in organisations where the people with the questions are not the people with SQL — operations managers, finance, a founder who wants to see weekly numbers without asking anyone. It loses where the sophistication of the modelling matters more than the accessibility of the front end.

Around the question builder sits the expected surface: dashboards with filters, auto-refresh and custom click behaviour; alerts on results and scheduled dashboard subscriptions out to email, Slack or a webhook; a semantic layer with a curated Library; transforms that turn raw tables into analytics-ready ones; embedding components for charts, dashboards and a data browser, up to putting an entire Metabase inside your own application; and granular permissions designed to work both for internal teams and for customer-facing embedded analytics.

The honest one-line summary

Metabase is the tool you choose when adoption is the constraint. If the real risk to your analytics project is that nobody outside the data team ever opens it, Metabase addresses that risk more directly than any of its competitors. If your real risk is modelling complexity, it does not.

02 · The licence split

0.x is AGPL. 1.x is commercial. Same release.

This is the single most misread fact about Metabase, and it decides procurement conversations.

Metabase's repository is dual-licensed by directory. Source outside the top-level enterprise directory is AGPL. Source inside it is under the Metabase Commercial License. The built artefacts follow the same split: images at metabase/metabase and the non-enterprise download paths are AGPL, while metabase/metabase-enterprise and the enterprise download paths are commercial.

The version numbers encode which one you are running. The open-source line is numbered 0.x and the commercial line 1.x, tracking each other release for release — as of 16 November 2026 the current pair is v0.65.4.4 and v1.65.4.4, published the same day. There are also long-term support lines, currently tagged v0.64-lts and an older v0.58-lts, which is the tag to pin if you would rather not chase minor releases.

What this means when you plan

Do not read a feature list and assume you get all of it. Metabase documents its product as a whole, and a meaningful part of the advanced surface — the areas most relevant to serious permissions and embedded analytics — lives behind the commercial licence. Before committing, list the five features your design depends on and confirm each one against the edition you intend to run. Pulling metabase/metabase because it is the obvious image name, then discovering a dependency on an enterprise feature, is the standard way this goes wrong.

The AGPL itself is worth a thought if you are embedding. Running Metabase internally carries no distribution obligation. Modifying it and offering it over a network to your customers is precisely what the AGPL's network clause addresses — which is, not coincidentally, the scenario the commercial licence exists to sell you out of.

03 · How it works

Questions, models, and the escape hatch.

Metabase connects directly to your database and queries it live; it is a query layer, not a storage engine, and it holds only its own application state. The progression a team moves through is consistent enough to plan for:

  • Questions. A saved query built in the UI or in SQL. The unit everything else is made of.
  • Models and the semantic layer. Curated, named starting points so that "revenue" means one thing. This is the step teams skip and then regret, because without it every department slowly invents its own definition of the same metric.
  • Dashboards. Questions arranged with shared filters, scheduled out to email or Slack, or alerting when a result crosses a threshold.
  • Embedding. Individual components or a whole Metabase inside your own product, with permissions that distinguish internal staff from each customer's own view of their own data.

Two newer capabilities are worth naming because they change the evaluation. Metabase ships an AI assistant and an agent API for querying data programmatically, and it supports versioning analytics work with git through its remote-sync feature — which, for anyone who has watched a BI estate rot into four hundred untracked saved questions, is the more interesting of the two.

04 · Against the 2nth default

Metabase vs Superset, without the diplomacy.

Superset is the default for new builds in this tree. Here is when that default is wrong.

DimensionMetabaseApache Superset
Primary userNon-technical staff asking their own questionsAnalysts and data engineers
LicenceAGPL core, commercial enterprise editionApache 2.0 throughout
GovernanceSingle vendorApache Software Foundation
Time to first dashboardShort — the onboarding is the productLonger — more assembly, more control
Chart depthAdequate; opinionated defaultsDeeper, more configurable
Commercial pressureReal — advanced features sit behind the paid editionNone in the licence

The trade is legible. Superset gives you one permissive licence and no vendor with an incentive to move a feature you depend on behind a paywall — which is why it is the default here. Metabase gives you adoption by people who would otherwise never open a BI tool, and that is worth a great deal in an organisation where the analytics project has failed twice already because nobody used it.

Choose Metabase when the human problem is larger than the technical one. Choose Superset when you have analysts, want everything under Apache 2.0, and would rather spend the setup time once than carry an edition question forever.

05 · Gotchas

What bites in production.

It queries your production database

Metabase's friendliness is also its risk: a curious user can write a question that performs badly against the live operational database at month-end. Point it at a replica or a warehouse, not the transactional primary, and set query limits before you hand out logins — not after someone explains why the invoicing screen was slow.

Saved-question sprawl is the default outcome

Low friction means volume. Without a semantic layer and some curation discipline, a year in you have hundreds of near-duplicate questions and no agreement on which is correct. Use models and the Library from the start; git-backed sync makes this tractable rather than aspirational.

The edition question never fully goes away

Because the OSS and commercial builds share a version number and a documentation site, it is easy to design against a feature you do not have. Keep a written note of which edition you run and which features you depend on.

Upgrades deserve the LTS line

Metabase ships frequently. If you do not want to track minor releases, pin an LTS tag and move deliberately. If you do track, read the release notes — this is an application that owns its own database and migrates it.

06 · Decision guide

Use it for, skip it for.

Use Metabase when

  • The people who need answers cannot write SQL and will not learn.
  • A previous BI rollout failed on adoption rather than capability.
  • You want dashboards and alerts running this week, not this quarter.
  • You are embedding analytics for customers and are willing to pay for the edition that supports it properly.
  • Your data already lives somewhere queryable and you need a front end, not a platform.

Skip Metabase when

  • A permissive licence is a hard requirement — Superset under Apache 2.0 is the cleaner answer.
  • Your users are analysts who will spend most of their time in SQL anyway. You are paying an adoption premium you do not need.
  • You need deeply custom visualisations. The chart library is deliberately opinionated.
  • Your reporting is operational monitoring of time-series infrastructure — that is Grafana's job, not this one.
  • You cannot tolerate a single-vendor dependency on the features you build around.
07 · South African context

Residency, rands and per-seat maths.

Self-hosting keeps the data home. Metabase queries your database directly and stores only its own application state, so a self-hosted instance on local infrastructure means your reporting layer adds no cross-border transfer at all. For an organisation that has decided to keep personal information in the Republic, this is a genuinely clean position — and a better one than most SaaS BI tools can offer. Metabase Cloud is a different conversation: convenient, and a POPIA section 72 transfer to document like any other.

Per-seat pricing is where rand budgets break. The commercial edition is priced in dollars, and BI is the category where seat counts creep — every manager who wants a dashboard is another seat. Model the cost at the headcount you will have in two years, not today, and convert at a rate you would defend to a board. For many South African mid-sized organisations the self-hosted AGPL edition on a local server is not a compromise; it is the correct answer, and the commercial edition only becomes compelling when customer-facing embedding enters the picture.

POPIA lands on the permissions model, not the tool. A BI front end is an access-control surface over personal information. The question an information officer will ask is not "which tool" but "who can see which rows, and can you show me". Design the permissions deliberately, keep the warehouse free of personal fields nobody needs for reporting, and remember that a dashboard emailed on a schedule is personal information leaving the system on a timer — subscriptions deserve the same scrutiny as logins.
